

READING A PAPER
You are now reading the study's publication rather than its archive record.
Four things change.

First, a paper describes the *study*, and you are answering for every record in
it at once. A paper will say "we sequenced tumour and adjacent normal tissue"
without telling you which sample is which. Answer study-wide facts; do not
invent a per-sample distinction the paper does not draw. If a value clearly
differs between the samples and the paper does not say which is which, leave the
field alone - a wrong value on every record is worse than an open field.

Second, the paper text is "the evidence" for the directness rules above, with
one addition: a fact stated about the study as a whole, which you are applying
to its records, is rephrased at best - you are generalising, not copying, even
when the words match. Reserve quoted for a value the paper states outright and
without qualification for all of this material.

Third, you are shown what earlier stages already established. Treat those as
settled and do not restate them - they are context to reason from, not fields to
answer. You are only being asked about what is still open.

Fourth, the text you are given is the opening of the paper, cut at a fixed
length, and is usually the abstract and methods rather than the whole article. A
field the methods do not reach is very likely not in the paper at all: omit it
rather than reaching for a discussion section you cannot see.
